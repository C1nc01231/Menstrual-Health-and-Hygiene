Understanding Menstrual Health and Hygiene

Menstruation is a natural and healthy part of life for millions of people around the world. Still, despite its commonality, periods are often surrounded by misconceptions, stereotypes, and limited access to reliable information and proper hygiene products.

This webpage aims to change that by providing a clear, science-based, and inclusive guidance about menstrual health, hygiene, products, and common concerns. Whether you are a student, educator, parent, or community member, this webpage is designed to help you understand, support, and discuss menstruation with confidence.

Menstruation, or a period, is the monthly process where the body releases blood and tissue when pregnancy does not occur. Each cycle, the uterus prepares a lining for a possible pregnancy—if none happens, that lining is shed through the vagina.

Most people get their first period between ages 11 and 14, and menstruation continues until menopause, around age 51. A period typically lasts 3 to 5 days.

During a period, it’s common to experience symptoms such as:

  • 1. in the abdomen or pelvis
  • 2. Lower back pain
  • 3. Bloating and breast tenderness
  • 4. Food cravings
  • 5. Mood swings or irritability
  • 6. Headaches and fatigue

Many of these signs also appear before the period starts, a group of symptoms known as Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS).

If someone notices major or sudden changes in their menstrual cycle, it’s important to consult a healthcare provider, as these changes may signal underlying health concerns.

Common Myths and Stereotypes About Menstruation

Myth: “Period blood is dirty or impure.”
Reality: Menstrual blood is just like any other bodily fluid it’s not dirty or morally impure.

Myth: “You shouldn’t bathe, shower, or wash during your period.”
Reality: Bathing and good hygiene are important; avoiding washing can increase the risk of infections.

Myth: “You shouldn’t exercise or swim during your period.”
Reality: Light to moderate exercise even swimming is safe and can help relieve cramps and improve mood.

Myth: “Everyone’s period works the same 28-day cycle, same flow, same symptoms.”
Reality: Menstrual cycles vary. Flow, cycle length, and symptoms differ widely between individuals.

Myth: “People on their period are emotionally unstable or overly moody.”
Reality: Hormones may influence mood, but it is inaccurate to stereotype menstruators as emotionally unstable.

Myth: “Menstruation should be kept a secret.”
Reality: Keeping periods secret reinforces stigma. Open conversations help normalize menstruation.

Myth: “If you're on birth control, you must get a period every month.”
Reality: Hormonal contraception often changes or pauses periods irregular cycles are common and not always unhealthy.

Myth: “Menstruation is shameful, impure, or a sign of weakness.”
Reality: Menstruation is a natural biological process and a sign of a healthy reproductive system.

What is Menstrual Cycle?

The menstrual cycle is a natural but complex cycle of changes that your body goes through each month. A menstrual cycle is measured from the first day of your period to the day before your next period. The average length of a menstrual cycle is 28 days, but everyone's cycle is different. For example, teenagers might have cycles that last 45 days, whereas people in their 20s to 30s might have cycles that last 21 to 38 days.

Menstruation (Days 1–5):

When you have a period, your uterus lining sheds and flows out of your vagina. Your period contains blood, mucus and some cells from the lining of your uterus. The average length of a period is 3 to 7 days.

Follicular Phase (Days 1–13):

The follicular phase starts on the first day of your period and lasts for 13 to 14 days. Changing hormone levels cause the lining of your uterus to thicken and follicles to grow on the surface of the ovaries. Usually only one follicle will mature into an egg.

Ovulation (Around Day 14):

Ovulation is when a mature egg is released from an ovary. This usually happens once a month, about 2 weeks before your next period. You're most likely to get pregnant if you have unprotected sex around the time of ovulation.

Luteal Phase (Days 15–28):

After ovulation, the egg travels through the fallopian tubes to your uterus. The uterus lining continues to thicken in preparation for pregnancy. If you fall pregnant, you won't have a period. If you don't get pregnant, you'll have a period and the menstrual cycle will start again.

Why Menstrual Health Matters?

Menstrual health matters because your period provides important clues about your overall reproductive and general health. Managing menstruation well, through hygiene, education, and proper care, helps prevent infections, reduces stress, and supports mental and physical well-being. It also empowers people to participate in school, work, and community life without being limited by period-related challenges. In this way, menstrual health is not just a personal issue; it’s essential for dignity, equality, and social inclusion.

Choosing the Right Menstrual Products

Choosing the right menstrual products is key to feeling comfortable, confident, and protected during your period. With so many options available today, from pads and tampons to menstrual cups and period underwear, it’s all about finding what suits your body, lifestyle, and personal preferences. Each product works in its own way, and knowing how they function helps you make safe, informed choices for your menstrual health.

To prevent irritation, infections, and discomfort, be sure to follow these important hygiene guidelines.

  • Use clean products: Sanitary pads, tampons, menstrual cups, or period underwear.
  • Change products every 4–8 hours.
  • Wash hands before and after handling period items.
  • Shower daily and use mild soap.
  • Stay hydrated, exercise, and eat balanced meals.
  • Dispose of products properly (wrap and throw away).

You can swim, exercise, or have sex during your period (use protection).

Other Healthy Habits for Managing Your Menstrual Cycle

• Track your cycle: Use an app or calendar.

• Eat balanced meals: Maintain proper nutrition.

• Sleep well: Keep a regular sleep routine.

• Stay active: Move your body but listen to its limits.

• Reduce stress: Practice relaxing habits.

Most period symptoms are normal and manageable:

1. Cramps (Dysmenorrhea)

Mild to moderate cramps are common as the uterus contracts to shed its lining.

How to relieve:

  • Use a heating pad
  • Take doctor-recommended pain relievers (like ibuprofen)
  • Try gentle stretches or light exercise

Seek help if cramps are so painful they stop you from doing daily activities — this may indicate conditions like endometriosis.

2. PMS (Premenstrual Syndrome)

PMS occurs due to hormonal changes before your period starts.

Common symptoms:

  • Mood swings or irritability
  • Bloating and breast tenderness
  • Fatigue
  • Headaches
  • Food cravings

If PMS severely affects your mood, relationships, or daily life, speak with a healthcare professional.

3. Irregular Periods

It’s normal for teens to have irregular cycles as the body adjusts to hormonal changes. Stress, sleep, nutrition, and medical conditions can also affect cycle length.

What’s normal:

  • Cycles between 21–35 days (sometimes longer for teens)
  • Occasional skipped periods due to stress or lifestyle changes

Seek advice if periods suddenly become very irregular, extremely frequent, or stop for several months (and you’re not pregnant).

4. Heavy Bleeding (Menorrhagia)

Some people naturally bleed more, but extremely heavy flow can signal an underlying issue.

Warning signs:

  • Soaking through a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours
  • Needing double protection (pad + tampon)
  • Passing large blood clots

Heavy bleeding needs medical evaluation to prevent anemia or detect underlying conditions.

5. When to Seek Medical Help

You should reach out to a healthcare professional if you notice:

1. Extremely Heavy Bleeding

  • Soaking through a pad or tampon every 1–2 hours
  • Passing large blood clots
  • Bleeding that lasts longer than 7 days

2. Severe or Unusual Pain

  • Cramps so intense they stop you from doing daily activities
  • Pain that does not get better with rest, heat, or medication

3. Sudden Changes in Your Cycle

  • Periods that become very irregular
  • Cycles that are much shorter or longer than usual
  • Periods stopping for 3+ months (and you’re not pregnant)

4. Signs of Infection

  • Foul-smelling discharge
  • Fever or chills
  • Severe itching or burning
  • Strong pelvic pain

5. Symptoms That Affect Daily Life

  • Mood swings, sadness, or anxiety that feel overwhelming
  • PMS or cramps that get worse over time
